    Roman numerals by measure

    One chord per beat, shown here and under the bass staff. Within each beat the analysis keeps a slice that already forms a triad or seventh chord; if none does, it drops the fewest pitches needed to leave one. Dropped pitches are treated as passing or neighbor tones and appear in the tooltip. A chord with a missing fifth or third is labeled as the complete chord. A suspension is labeled as the chord it resolves into, with its figures, as in V(4–3). Under a bass note held through a change of harmony, the labels are joined by a dash, as in ii64–V. A ? marks a beat that fits no single chord, so its label is a guess.

    Minor keys use natural-minor numerals with the raised sixth and seventh unmarked, so the subtonic chord is VII and the dominant is V. Chromatic chords that tonicize the next harmony are labeled as applied chords. A major triad or dominant seventh whose root falls a fifth to the next chord is V/x, as in V6/vi in BWV 253, m. 6. A diminished chord whose root rises a half step is vii°/x. A chromatic chord that resolves some other way keeps its own label, so a closing Picardy third stays I. Parallel fifths and octaves are checked between sung voices only; treat them as places to look in the score, not verdicts. Fermatas play as written and are followed by an eighth-rest breath. Playback follows repeat signs and first and second endings, and tied notes sound as one.
